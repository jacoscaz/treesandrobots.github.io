## 0. Obligatory disclaimer: I am not a trainer

I am not a trainer. I am not formally qualified to teach fitness classes or 
provide any kind of fitness coaching. Take what follows with a grain of salt,
do your own research, be responsible for your own choices.

## 1. The underlying goal: fitness in service of life

Fitness isn't about maximizing athletic performance, building the biggest
muscles or achieving the best measurable scores.

Fitness is about maintaining a healthy, strong, capable body throughout life.
In my specific case, this also means counteracting the effects of a sedentary
and intellectually demanding profession.

Fitness, then, means:

- Maintaining meaningful levels of strength and cardiovascular fitness.
- Preserving mobility, flexibility, coordination and physical competence.
- Developing sufficient physical reserves to make everyday activities comfortable.
- Reducing the negative consequences of repetitive daily habits.
- Making physical activity something enjoyable enough to sustain for decades.

> An important premise is that physical strength is an essential component of
> health and longevity, yet frequently underestimated.

## 2. From exercise to physical practice

**Exercise** is generally instrumental: the trainee performs an activity
to elicit a particular physiological adaptation. **Physical practice** is a
much broader concept predicated on nurturing and deepening the relationship
between the practitioner and their body, with all of its capabilities and 
limitations.

Consider the difference:

- Doing pull-ups to improve pulling strength.
- Climbing a tree because climbing is fun.
- Stretching to improve mobility.
- Exploring unfamiliar movements because discovering what the body can do is intrinsically rewarding.
- Practicing breathing to develop awareness and physiological self-regulation.

While these activities can produce overlapping benefits, how we relate to them
is psychologically quite different. Physical practice should be a natural and
enjoyable part of everyday life and not something to be endured.

## 3. The three layers of physical practice

There are three layers to this philosophy:

**Layer 1: non-negotiable principles**

This layer establishes the boundaries within which the practitioner must
operate:

- Cover the fundamental movement patterns.
- Develop and maintain strength.
- Maintain cardiovascular fitness.
- Preserve mobility and coordination.
- Manage fatigue and recovery.
- Avoid accumulating physical imbalances.

At this level, it is extremely important to recognize that most practitioners
are neither athletes nor experts in physical fitness and often do not have
access to professional guidance and/or equipment on a daily basis. Physical
practice must evaluate risk factors and adapt to the practitioner's unique
needs.

**Layer 2: loose organizational structure**

This layer provides consistency without requiring excessive planning:

- A reasonably predictable weekly rhythm.
- Push and pull movement emphases.
- Different lower-body movement patterns.
- Planned conditioning and recovery opportunities.
- Sufficient regularity to develop and maintain physical capacities.

**Layer 3: freedom of execution**

This layer provides enjoyment and spontaneity, which are key to the long-term
sustainability of physical practice:

- Exercise variations.
- Improvisation.
- Play and exploration.
- Adjustments based on how the practitioner feels.
- Novel movements and activities.
- Freedom to stop when the practitioner has had enough.

---

These layers serve different purposes:

- The first layer protects the integrity of the practice.
- The second layer reduces decision fatigue and helps manage training load.
- The third layer preserves enjoyment, curiosity and intrinsic motivation.

Ultimately, however, these three layers stem from a single overarching
principle:

> The purpose of structure is to create the conditions for freedom, not to
> constrain it.

This principle applies to physical practice and countless other domains. It 
should feel intimately familiar to anyone who has spent a significant amount
of time in any kind of engineering context.

## 4. Tracking, monitoring and regulating

As concepts, tracking, monitoring and regulating are obviously related to one
another. However, that does not make them interchangeable:

- **Tracking** is the recording of objective, measurable metrics: repetitions,
  weights, duration, heart rate and so on.
- **Monitoring** is the attention paid to performance, fatigue, discomfort,
  recovery and motivation. 
- **Regulating** is the adjustment of training based on the information
  available.

**Tracking is too often mistaken for monitoring and awareness.**

Physical practice is about developing awareness of one's own state such that 
the practitioner can safely and reliably self-monitor and self-regulate even
without the help of pen and paper, spreadsheets, fitness trackers and other
such tools. It is about recognizing when to push, when to back off, when to
replace an exercise and when to stop without the aid of someone or something 
external to ourselves.

That said, tracking and monitoring are not mutually exclusive. Awareness isn't
infallible, after all, and accumulated fatigue isn't always obvious. Awareness 
also needs to be trained and developed; tracking can be a tremendous help in
doing so. However, tracking through external tools is a stepping stone towards
awareness and not a replacement for it.

## 5. A system designed around sustainability

Adding a highly structured, demanding regimen to a life already full of
substantial demands, prolonged sitting and relatively little spontaneous
physical activity, can quickly become counterproductive.

A physical practice regimen cannot assume fitness to be the central priority
around which everything else ought to revolve. Instead, it must be designed
accounting for the constraints of the practitioner's life. This is the only
way to ensure that physical practice remains sustainable and effective.

To this end, physical practice should maintain a balance between two
complementary dimensions:

- **Deliberate physical practice**: strength, conditioning, mobility,
  coordination and recovery.
- **Everyday movement**: walking, climbing, carrying, changing positions,
  playing, dancing and spending time outdoors.

The second is particularly important. Exercise sessions should not be the only
occasions on which the practitioner engages physically with the world.

## 6. Physical practice as a lifelong investment

The goal of physical practice is to develop and maintain the capabilities that
allow you to enjoy life as your circumstances and body change over time. Doing 
so implies maintaining:

- Strength and muscle mass.
- Cardiovascular capacity.
- Bone health.
- Balance and coordination.
- Mobility and usable range of motion.
- Physical confidence and independence.

Therefore, a physical practice regimen must evolve as the practitioner ages.
Exercises, intensity and the balance between different activities must change
in service of the desire to move as freely as possible for as long as possible.

## 7. Summing it all up

1. Physical fitness is a lifelong practice, not a temporary project or an
   exercise in performance optimization.
2. Build enough structure to develop and maintain the physical capabilities
   that matter. Keep that structure flexible enough to accommodate curiosity,
   spontaneity and enjoyment.
3. Monitor your body, regulate your effort, and don't confuse tracking with
   awareness.
4. Develop strength, cardiovascular fitness, mobility and coordination not
   merely to perform better at exercise, but to remain physically capable in
   everyday life.

**Above all, make movement something to look forward to. The ultimate measure
of success is not how well the practitioner follows a program but how naturally
physical activity becomes a part of their life.**

## 8. Where does this all come from?

My interest in fitness started relatively early, in my early teenage years.

I started out with basic calisthenics during physical education class, which
at my school was unfortunately taught rather unseriously. Doing so helped me
develop confidence in my own body, in my ability to achieve physical goals and
to do my own thing without caring too much about the judgment of others.

Eventually I started complementing calisthenics with free weights (think Mark
Rippetoe's [Starting Strength]), which taught me the value of structured
progression. Then I got into [CrossFit], broadening my understanding of physical
preparedness. I got my CrossFit Level I certification in 2013. I later came to
question CrossFit's rigidity and intensity, bringing further separation between
the strength and metabolic conditioning elements of my workouts. 

Later, after struggling with integrating physical practice into my adult life,
I rediscovered the joy of unstructured movement, and explored natural movement,
mobility, mindfulness and breathwork. You can find my [fitness routine] on
GitHub.
